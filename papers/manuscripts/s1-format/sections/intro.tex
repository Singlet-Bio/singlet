\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Single-cell studies now routinely profile hundreds of thousands to millions of cells, reference atlases hold tens of
millions~\cite{svensson2018exponential,sikkema2023integrated,czi2025cellxgene}, and a single perturbation screen has
reached a hundred million~\cite{zhang2025tahoe}. These data are no longer only analysed once on a laptop: they are served
by atlases to thousands of users and streamed, minibatch by minibatch and epoch after epoch, into machine-learning models
that read every cell many times~\cite{lopez2018deep,theodoris2023transfer,cui2024scgpt}. More and more often they live in
cloud object storage and travel over a network before they are used.

At the centre of all of this is one kind of object: a sparse matrix of whole-number counts, genes by cells, in which most
entries are zero and most of the rest are small. Yet the formats in use, AnnData~\cite{virshup2024anndata}, the 10x
Genomics HDF5 file, loom, Zarr and TileDB-SOMA~\cite{tiledbsoma}, store it as generic arrays and compress them with generic
tools such as gzip or zstd, which see only bytes and know nothing about counts. The files are therefore several times
larger than they need to be, and when data travel over a network, the size of the file and the number of requests needed to
reach a few hundred cells, not the model, decide how fast training can go.

Count matrices are far from arbitrary. Most non-zero counts are exactly one. How deeply a cell was sequenced sets the
expected count of every gene in it. And if genes are numbered from most to least frequently detected, the positions of
a cell's non-zero entries become a short list of small steps. A compressor that knows these regularities can describe the
same matrix in far fewer bits, as domain-specific formats for sequencing reads did when they replaced gzip with models of
their data~\cite{bonfield2022cram}. The challenge is to do so while decoding fast enough that the smaller file is also the
faster one, and while still letting a reader fetch any subset of cells directly from a remote file.

Here we present \sone, an open format for single-cell count matrices built around these regularities, and the
\texttt{singlet} package that reads and writes it from Python, R and C++. We compare \sone{} with every widely used format
on \nm{nMatrices} public matrices spanning droplet, plate, combinatorial, multimodal and spatial protocols, and measure what
matters in practice: how much storage a matrix takes, how fast it can be read, how quickly a training loader receives
minibatches from local disk, a shared filesystem and cloud storage, and what that costs. We find that \sone{} files are the
smallest on every matrix and among the fastest to read, that a loader reading \sone{} keeps up with a typical network link,
and that the compression is close to what the best count models we fitted would allow.
